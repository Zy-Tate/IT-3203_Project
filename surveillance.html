Data Harvesting & Surveillance

How everyday internet use is tracked and monetized.

Commercial Tracking & Data Brokers

Every time someone browses the web, uses an app on their phone, or clicks a link, digital trackers log their activity. Many companies put invisible code, pixels used for tracking, and software development kits, called SDKs, into websites and apps to track a user's behavior. These tools look at and keep track of visited URLs, searches, locations, and time spent on a specific page. Instead of operating as separate, isolated records, this info feeds real time advertising exchanges that analyzes a person’s habits to give them targeted advertisements.

Data brokers take this surveillance even farther by doing their operations completely behind the scenes without direct contact with consumers. These companies like to purchase and compile information from a huge amount of different sources. They combine online records with offline info like credit card purchase records, store loyalty programs, voting registries, etc. By connecting separate data points from things like an email address or device ID, data brokers create in depth personal profiles on millions of customers around the world.

The consequences of this unregulated market go farther than relevant advertisements. Data brokers sell access to consumer dossiers to banks, insurance providers, employers, and government agencies. These profiles can potentially influence the interest rate a broker will give to somebody, the insurance premiums a driver has to pay, or even the employment opportunities a job applicant can get access to. Because consumers don’t often consent to secondary trade and lack tools to opt out, data brokerage turns people’s private life into a product, sold to the highest bidder.


Network & ISP Logging

An Internet Service Provider, or ISP, is the physical gateway for all the incoming and outgoing internet traffic. Even when websites use the HTTPS encryption to protect passwords and messages, the connection is never completely invisible. In order to route data correctly, the network still needs to read destination IP addresses and process Domain Name System, or DNS, requests. This pretty much means while an ISP cannot read the specific text on an encrypted page, it still records every primary domain a person visits, with exact timestamps, bandwidth volume, and duration of the sessions.

This accumulated network meta data provides a very detailed timeline of a person’s daily life. By analyzing the patterns of when and where a device connects, which server(s) it makes contact with, and how much data it may move, providers can use that information to infer personal routines. These records can be used to reveal work schedules, streaming habits, and the types of mobile apps that are active on home networks. In many jurisdictions, corporate policies permit broadband providers to share or sell behavioral insights to commercial advertisers and data brokers without any consumer intervention.

Government regulations further support this logging process. Telecommunication laws in a lot of countries require the internet providers to keep customer connection history and IP assignment logs for multiple months or even years to satisfy potential requests from law enforcement. Even turning on private browsing mode or incognito mode on a browser only wipes local history from the personal computer, but it does nothing to stop the upstream network from archiving the connection. Without secondary tools like Virtual Private Networks, VPNs, or encrypted DNS protocols, an ISP will always record online activity of citizens.
